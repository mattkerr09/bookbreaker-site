Does a player's streak hold up in the next games?

In this NFL player study, spots showing an 80% or higher last-5 rate averaged 82% in those past games. The later rate was 56%: 482 of 860 games, with a Wilson 95% range of 53% to 59%. The past count was right. It described those games, without settling what came next.

The gap between the display and the next games

I checked player yards rather than team points. The question was simple: after a player reached his own number in most recent games, how often did he reach that same number in the games after? The number stayed fixed throughout the check. No 2025 result helped choose it.

The later rate divides all reaches by all later games, so a spot with five later games adds more than one with only one.

How I chose the number and the spots

The eligible group was 274 quarterbacks, running backs, wide receivers and tight ends. Each had at least eight regular-season games in 2024 and a rounded-up 2024 median of at least 10 yards. The main stat was passing yards for quarterbacks, rushing yards for running backs, and receiving yards for receivers and tight ends. Each player and position pair had its own log.

For each log, I sorted the games by season and week. I took the middle 2024 yardage value, averaging the two middle values when needed, then rounded up to a whole yard. That was the player's number for the whole study. A game equal to the number counted as reaching it. A blank yards value in a recorded row counted as zero.

I then walked through 2025 in order. A spot was a game where the rate across the five or ten played games ending there fell in a band. Early lookbacks could include the end of 2024. The outcome used only the next five recorded games from 2025. At season end I kept fewer than five when that was all that remained. A spot with none was left out.

After a spot, the next spot in that player's same band was at least six games later. Its five later games could not overlap the earlier spot's five later games. Each band ran separately. I pooled the later counts across players and used Wilson 95% ranges with z = 1.96. Percentages and range ends are rounded to whole numbers.

What the gap means

The past count is right: it describes those past games. In the last-10 check, the shown rate ran above the later rate in every band from 40% up. In the last-5 check, that held from 60% up. The exception was 40 to 59%: 40% shown became 42% later. The under-40% band ran below the later rate in both windows.

Part of this pattern is regression toward each player's usual level. Choosing unusually high recent counts can select runs that include good fortune. The next games need not keep that same share. The reverse can happen after low counts. This check does not measure how much each cause contributed.

That is why my plan for BookBreaker pairs the count with a range and a later-games check. This is one season, one league and yards only. Players can share games and conditions. The pooled Wilson ranges use a model of separate trials with a common rate; they do not account for every connection between these results.

What it does not mean

This makes no claim about any single player or game. It does not predict future results and gives no advice. A pooled rate belongs to this group and these rules. It is not a personal forecast or a reason to expect the same outcome in a different season.

Questions

Did 82% turn into 56% for every player?

No. Those are group summaries. The 82% is the average shown across 199 spots. The 56% pools 482 reaches across 860 later games.

Did the 2025 games choose the number?

No. Only the 2024 regular-season yards chose each number. The 2025 rows supplied the spots and their later outcomes.

Why are there fewer than five later games per spot on average?

Spots near the end of 2025 can have one to four later games. Those shorter groups stay in the count, with their actual number of games.

Is this proof that a streak cannot continue?

No. It measures what followed these past spots. It does not settle the next result or identify a particular player.
