Is a 10-game trend reliable?

Ten games can describe what just happened, but leave a wide range for the underlying rate. Five games leave even more doubt. Twenty can narrow the range without settling what happens next. Read the count, the range and the game context together. None of them promises that a recent trend will continue.

The same rate can carry different weight

A short sample can look neat because a percentage is neat. The count behind it is the part to read first. The table holds the observed share steady and changes how many games it came from. The wider range leaves more room for the underlying rate. Adding games can narrow that range when the share stays the same. It does not make the next game certain. A whole season can also mix quite different conditions. More rows help with sampling doubt; they do not prove that the situation stayed the same.

How I work out the range

I use the Wilson score interval. Divide the number of reaches, k, by the games played, n, to get the observed share p. Use z = 1.96 for the 95% interval. Square z. Add z squared divided by twice n to p, then divide by one plus z squared divided by n. That gives the adjusted centre.

For the half width, take the square root of p times one minus p divided by n, plus z squared divided by four times n squared. Multiply that root by z, then divide by one plus z squared divided by n. Subtract the half width from the centre for the low end. Add it for the high end. The table rounds both ends to whole percentages after doing the calculation.

This model treats games as separate trials with a common underlying rate. Real games may not fit that assumption. Related opponents, changed roles and different conditions can matter. A 95% interval is a method that would cover the fixed rate in about 95% of repeated samples under that model. It is not a 95% promise about the next game.

Detroit puts the range in context

In the local file, Detroit reached 24 or more points in 12 of 17 games in its 2025 regular season. That is 71% after rounding. Its Wilson range runs from 47% to 87%. This example uses team points. For players, the NFL streak study counts what came next.

A large-looking season percentage can therefore sit beside a wide interval. Read both. I would also inspect the dated rows before trying to explain the count. The calculation tells you how much sampling doubt remains under its assumptions. It does not tell you why those scores happened.

Choose the window before the result

Use a short window to ask about a recent stretch. Use the season to ask about the season. Put them next to one another if both questions matter. Moving between windows until one looks striking gives you a different sort of search. A range around that chosen result does not account for every other result you looked at along the way.

Questions

Are 20 games always enough?

No fixed window settles every question. A longer window can contain older conditions. Check what those games represent as well as how many there are. A narrower range still rests on the model used to make it.

Does a wide range make the count wrong?

No. The count can be exact for those games while the underlying rate remains uncertain. Those are different statements. Keep the observed count and the estimated range together.

Can a later-games check remove the doubt?

It adds evidence from another period. It still needs its own count, range and clear rules for choosing past spots. Later results must not decide which earlier spots get into the study.
